## The mechanism, briefly

For a typical apparel, food or electronics brand, the large majority of total emissions sit in **Scope 3 category 1: purchased goods and services** — which means, in practice, the supply chain. A brand cannot credibly report or reduce its own footprint without supplier-level data, because supplier data *is* most of the number.

That is the entire reason a factory in Bangladesh, Vietnam or India suddenly finds itself filling in a detailed emissions questionnaire it was never asked for five years ago.

## What is driving the timing

**Regulation on the buyer's side.** CSRD in the EU requires large companies to disclose Scope 3 emissions with assurance, which pushes the data requirement down the chain to suppliers who were previously asked for nothing more specific than a sustainability policy statement.

**Voluntary target frameworks tightening.** SBTi validation and CDP disclosure both increasingly expect primary supplier data rather than industry-average emission factors, particularly for brands renewing or upgrading their targets.

**Buyer scorecards formalising.** Programmes such as Higg or Cascale reporting are moving from qualitative sustainability scoring toward quantitative emissions data as a scored line item, which changes supplier engagement from "nice to have" to commercially material.

## What "good" looks like from a buyer's side

A buyer's sustainability team is not looking for a perfect number. They are looking for:

- Data collected at the **facility level**, not extrapolated from an industry average
- A **methodology they can cite** in their own disclosure without a caveat
- A supplier who can **answer follow-up questions** rather than hand over a PDF and go quiet

## The risk in doing nothing

Buyers who cannot get usable data from a supplier increasingly have somewhere else to go. The commercial risk is not a fine — it is being quietly deprioritised in favour of a supplier who made the data request easy to answer.
